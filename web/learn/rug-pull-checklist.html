A rug pull checklist: three questions before you buy any meme coin

Almost every rug pull needs one of three things: a creator bag, liquidity someone can pull, or code someone can change. How to check for each, and what Landing Pad rules out.

A rug pull is when the people behind a coin take the money and leave. The chart drops to near zero in minutes, and holders are left with coins nobody will buy.

Rug pulls come in many forms, but almost all of them need at least one of three things. If you check for these three before you buy, you rule out most of them. This checklist works for any meme coin, on any launchpad.

1. Is there a creator bag?

The question: does the creator, or a group of wallets close to the creator, hold a large share of the supply that they got for free or very cheaply?

Why it matters: a large bag can be sold at any time into the same market everyone else depends on. Even without bad intent, a creator holding 10% or 20% of the supply has a strong reason to sell into the first big rise.

How to check:

  • Look at the list of top holders on a block explorer. Exclude the pool or curve contract itself.
  • Look at the first transactions after launch. Did a handful of wallets buy a large share in the first block or two? Were those wallets funded from the same source?
  • Read the launch details. Is there a "team allocation", "dev share" or presale?

On Landing Pad: the creator gets zero coins at launch. There is no team allocation and no presale. The first 800M coins sell at one fixed price to whoever buys, and no wallet can hold more than 2.5% of the supply. The creator earns 1% of trading fees instead.

2. Can someone pull the liquidity?

The question: where is the money that backs the coin, and can any person withdraw it?

Why it matters: this is the classic rug. The money sits in a liquidity pool, the creator holds the pool tokens, and one transaction empties it. After that the coin has no buyers and no price.

How to check:

  • Find the liquidity pool for the coin. Who holds the LP tokens?
  • Are the LP tokens burned, or locked in a contract with a clear unlock date you can read on-chain?
  • Is there any function, on the coin or the pool, that lets an owner or admin move funds?

On Landing Pad: each coin's money sits in its own reserve, filled by the sale. The reserve has no withdraw function. The only way money leaves it is when a holder sells coins back at the floor. The platform's reserve and treasury wallets are locked by the contracts. The one wallet the team controls is the platform fee wallet, which receives the launchpad's 2% of each trade.

3. Can someone change the code?

The question: can the contract be upgraded, paused, or given new rules after you buy?

Why it matters: a contract that can be changed can be changed to block selling, add a large tax, mint new coins, or send funds somewhere new. "Honeypot" coins, which let you buy but not sell, often work this way.

How to check:

  • On Ethereum, check whether the contract is a proxy, and who owns it. Look for functions like pause, blacklist, setFee or mint that only an owner can call.
  • On Solana, check whether the program has an upgrade authority, and whether the token's mint and freeze authorities have been removed.
  • Try a small sell yourself before you buy more. If you cannot sell, stop.

On Landing Pad: the rules are set in the contracts, and the same rules apply to every coin: the fixed sale, the curve, the 5% fee split, the 2.5% wallet cap and the floor that is never lowered. Every change to the project is made in public, with its tests, on GitHub. The contracts are not audited yet, and the project is still in public test, so treat any claim about code, including ours, as something to verify, not something to trust.

Two more checks worth a minute

Who is talking about it? Brand new accounts posting the same message, sudden waves of replies, and promises of a "guaranteed 100x" are signs of paid promotion. Real projects can be hyped too, but a promise of returns is always a warning.

What is the worst case? Ask what you get back if everyone else sells at once. On most launchpads the answer is "whatever the next buyer pays." On Landing Pad it is the floor, less the 5% fee: $1,000 in and straight out returns $902.50. Knowing the worst case before you buy is the single best habit in this market.

The checklist

Before you buy any meme coin:

  1. Creator bag? Check top holders and the first blocks.
  2. Pullable liquidity? Check who holds or controls the pool.
  3. Changeable code? Check owners, upgrade authority, and mint or freeze authority.
  4. Paid hype? Ignore anyone promising returns.
  5. Worst case? Know what you get back if everyone sells.

None of this guarantees a coin will do well. It only rules out the most common ways to lose everything at once.
